Current Cadastral Trends
Land is a finite resource that is essential for economic development, agriculture, environmental management, urban planning, food security, and social equity. As competition for land increases, societies need reliable ways to identify, manage, and disseminate information about land and property. This function is performed by a land administration system (LAS), which defines and manages interests in land, including rights connected with water, air, and space. Land administration involves identifying, recording, and disseminating information about ownership, value, and use of land and related resources. An effective LAS supports the property market, land management, land registration, land valuation, taxation, policy-making, and the Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs).

At the centre of an LAS is the cadastre, which records information about land parcels and their legal and spatial characteristics. Although cadastral systems were originally developed mainly for taxation, their role has expanded considerably. Modern cadastres must respond to increasingly complex relationships between people and land, including activities vertically above and below the surface and in marine areas. They now contribute to environmental protection, climate-change adaptation, disaster risk management, public services, food security, and sustainable development. A modern LAS therefore requires clear information about rights, restrictions, and responsibilities (RRRs) and increasingly relies on thematic cadastral approaches that represent particular aspects of land administration. The following six trends illustrate how cadastral systems are evolving.

3D Cadastres
Urban growth has created increasingly complex vertical spaces in which different rights and interests exist above and below the land surface. Multi-storey building developments, underground infrastructure, and the use of airspace create overlapping property interests that cannot be adequately represented by a traditional two-dimensional (2D) cadastre. A 3D cadastre addresses this limitation by recording property rights and restrictions in three dimensions and by representing property units as volumes above, below, or on the ground. These volumes can be treated as "3D parcels” and can be linked to legal documents such as a notarial deed and information contained in the land and mortgage register or other forms of registry.

3D cadastral systems can represent a wide range of rights, including condominium ownership, easement rights, or a right of superficies. They can therefore help define the spatial extent of individual ownership and shared interests in complex developments, resolve boundaries, and clarify relationships between a co-owner and other parties. They are particularly relevant where several property units occupy the same building or where underground and above-ground spaces are subject to different rights and restrictions.

The development of 3D cadastres depends heavily on accurate 3D data models, visualisation, and standardisation. The Land Administration Domain Model (LADM) is particularly important because it provides a conceptual framework for representing the legal and administrative dimensions of land administration. Technologies such as Building Information Modelling (BIM) and CityGML provide detailed spatial representation of buildings and urban environments. Their integration can connect legal information with physical objects. BIM can, for example, represent condominium units and building components, while CityGML can support urban-scale visualisation. Remote sensing, LiDAR, airborne laser scanning, and digital-twin technologies can further improve data collection and visualisation.

An important future objective is to develop interoperable systems in which cadastral, building, planning, and registration information can be connected. Despite considerable technical progress, however, legal, registration, and organisational aspects of 3D cadastres remain less developed than the technical dimension. Issues such as the definition of 3D property rights, easement, registration procedures, valuation, taxation, liability, and the relationship between physical structures and ownership records require further development.

Marine and Coastal Cadastres
The growing use of marine and coastal space has created another important field of land administration: the marine and coastal cadastre. Marine areas support fishing, agriculture, transport, tourism, energy production, mining, environmental conservation, and other economic activities. As these uses expand, different rights and interests increasingly overlap across coastal waters, the seabed, shorelines, estuaries, and other marine spaces.

A marine cadastre can record information about rights, restrictions, responsibilities, and their spatial boundaries, helping to prevent conflicts and establish legal certainty. It can also support the management of competing interests and provide information needed for Marine Spatial Planning (MSP). Reliable marine spatial data infrastructure (MSDI) is important because authorities and other users require accessible, high-quality, and interoperable information to manage marine resources and prepare spatial plans.

Marine cadastral information can include ownership or other forms of tenure, licences, leases, environmental restrictions, fishing rights, infrastructure rights, and other interests. The challenge is that marine rights are often governed by different institutions and legal frameworks, making integration and standardisation difficult. Greater attention is therefore needed not only to the spatial representation of marine rights, but also to their legal status, land valuation and marine valuation, registration, governance, and relationships between public and private interests.

Fit-for-Purpose Land Administration (FFPLA)
In many developing countries, formal land administration systems remain incomplete, while insecure land tenure leaves millions of people vulnerable to eviction, disputes, and economic instability. Traditional LASs can be expensive, slow, and technically demanding to establish and maintain. Fit-for-purpose land administration (FFPLA) provides an alternative approach based on solutions that are practical, flexible, inclusive, participatory, affordable, and capable of continuous improvement.

Rather than requiring highly sophisticated cadastral procedures from the beginning, FFPLA adapts methods to local conditions while maintaining an appropriate level of accuracy. The approach can use drones, satellite imagery, handheld GNSS, mobile phones, and crowdsourced data to collect and update information about land. These technologies can make land registration faster and more affordable, particularly in areas where conventional surveying and registration procedures are difficult to implement.

FFPLA is not limited to recording ownership and tenure. It can also support land valuation, land consolidation, climate-change management, forest protection, housing resilience, taxation, waste management, and the resolution of land disputes. Its principle of improvability allows cadastral information to be progressively upgraded as financial resources, institutional capacity, and technology improve.

FFPLA can therefore contribute to poverty reduction, food security, gender equality, good governance, and the Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs). Its participatory character can also help incorporate local knowledge and information about informal or customary rights that may not be present in formal ownership records. At the same time, such systems require appropriate legal recognition and institutional arrangements so that recorded information can provide meaningful tenure security and support the wider property market.

Technical Infrastructure Cadastre
The increasing use of underground space has created a need for a technical infrastructure cadastre or utility cadastre. Modern cities depend on extensive underground networks containing electricity cables, water and gas pipelines, telecommunications infrastructure, sewage and drainage systems, underground transport, parking facilities, shopping centres, and other structures.

Information about these facilities is often incomplete, outdated, or held separately by different utilities and public authorities. This can lead to accidents, construction delays, financial losses, disputes, and difficulties in determining responsibility for damage. A technical infrastructure cadastre can record information about both the physical characteristics and legal status of underground infrastructure, including relevant RRRs.

Because underground utilities occupy different levels of the same physical space, 3D approaches are particularly useful. 3D models can represent layered underground environments and show the spatial relationship between land parcels, buildings, utility networks, tunnels, and other structures. Building Information Modelling (BIM), CityGML, and the Land Administration Domain Model (LADM) can contribute to the integration of physical, spatial, and legal information.

An effective utility cadastre should ideally be interoperable with the wider LAS, planning systems, construction information, and utility databases. However, important legal and economic aspects remain insufficiently developed. These include liability, insurance, taxation, valuation, ownership, easement rights, access rights, and responsibility for maintaining or relocating infrastructure. Clear information is particularly important where underground infrastructure crosses property boundaries or occupies space subject to different ownership and easement arrangements.

Cadastre of Public Law Restrictions (PLRs)
Property rights are not unlimited. Governments impose public law restrictions (PLRs) to protect the public interest and regulate how land may be used. These restrictions may relate to environmental protection, cultural heritage, mining, urban planning, public services, infrastructure, natural-resource conservation, or other regulatory objectives.

PLRs are often administered by different institutions and recorded in separate databases. As a result, information about restrictions may be fragmented and difficult for property owners, potential purchasers, planners, surveyors, and public authorities to access. A dedicated PLR cadastre or registry can record and disseminate this information in a systematic and transparent manner.

The spatial representation of PLRs can be particularly challenging because some restrictions extend over complex areas or have a three-dimensional character. Integration with a 3D cadastre can help show how restrictions affect particular parcels, buildings, underground spaces, or other property units. This can improve transparency and provide a clearer understanding of the relationship between private ownership and public regulation.

Countries such as Switzerland, Spain, and Slovenia have developed advanced approaches to the registration and dissemination of PLRs. Nevertheless, further work is needed to achieve effective standardisation and interoperability between institutions. A comprehensive PLR system should make it possible to identify relevant restrictions when land is transferred, developed, valued, mortgaged, or otherwise used. This is particularly important for the property market, where undisclosed or poorly understood restrictions can affect the use and value of property.

Disaster-Responsive Cadastre
Cadastral information is also increasingly important for disaster risk management (DRM). Earthquakes, floods, hurricanes, landslides, wildfires, and other hazards can destroy buildings, alter landscapes, displace populations, and create uncertainty about land and property rights. Reliable information about ownership, use of land, property values, and physical development is therefore essential before, during, and after a disaster.

Before a disaster, cadastral and land-use information can support hazard assessment, spatial planning, prevention, and housing resilience. During an emergency, accurate spatial information can assist rescue, evacuation, relief operations, and the management of critical infrastructure. After a disaster, ownership records and cadastral information can help resolve boundaries and identify affected property, while land valuation can support damage assessment, compensation, reconstruction, and insurance claims.

Disaster-responsive cadastres connect land administration with all stages of DRM: risk reduction, preparedness, emergency response, recovery, and reconstruction. Notarial deeds, ownership information, and registration records can be particularly important after resettlement, helping to prevent disputes over property rights. Land-use information can support the planning of safer housing and infrastructure, while property values can provide a basis for compensation and financial recovery.

Informal tenure requires particular attention because people without formal registration may face difficulties proving their rights after a disaster. A disaster-responsive LAS should therefore be capable of incorporating different forms of tenure and relevant evidence, rather than relying exclusively on formal title documents. Reliable, up-to-date, and accessible cadastral information can reduce uncertainty and support more equitable recovery.

Conclusion
Modern land administration is no longer limited to recording surface parcels for taxation. It has evolved into a multidimensional information infrastructure that connects legal rights and restrictions with physical space, buildings, infrastructure, marine environments, public regulation, and disaster risks.

The six trends discussed—3D cadastre, marine and coastal cadastre, fit-for-purpose land administration, technical infrastructure or utility cadastre, cadastre of public law restrictions, and Disaster-responsive cadastres—demonstrate how LASs are adapting to increasingly complex social, economic, urban, and environmental conditions. Across these fields, the ability to record information, disseminate information, resolve boundaries, and connect legal and spatial data is becoming increasingly important.

Modern technologies such as GIS, remote sensing, LiDAR, BIM, 3D modelling, mobile data collection, crowdsourced data, and AI can improve cadastral data collection, visualisation, and standardisation. However, technology alone cannot create an effective LAS. Sustainable cadastral development also requires appropriate legislation, reliable registries, capable institutions, clear ownership and tenure arrangements, interoperable databases, and governance frameworks that can adapt to changing needs.
