Modern developments in laser-scanning technology have created new possibilities for surveying, geodetic control and three-dimensional modelling of engineering structures. Reliable monitoring is particularly important for linear structures such as roads, because changes in their geometry or spatial position may indicate deformation and affect their durability and safety. Traditional geodetic methods can provide accurate measurements, but they may give only limited information about the position of individual parts of a large object. Terrestrial laser scanning (TLS) offers a different approach by collecting a dense set of spatial measurements that can be used to reconstruct the actual shape and position of a structure.

The proposed surveying technique is based on the use of a terrestrial laser scanner to obtain a three-dimensional model of a linear engineering object. The model can then be compared with a similar model obtained at a later time, making it possible to identify changes in geometry and spatial position. The method includes horizontal and vertical control, reconnaissance of the object, selection of suitable scanning stations, determination of the scanning distance and subsequent processing of the collected data. Special spheres with a known diameter are used as reference objects. Their centres can be identified automatically during processing, while tribrachs mounted on tripods allow spheres and reflectors to be exchanged in order to reduce centering errors.

The process begins at the design stage, when the required accuracy, volume of work, equipment and method of presenting the final product are established. Information about the initial geodetic network is collected, after which the main horizontal and vertical survey network is created with satellite equipment or electronic tacheometers. During reconnaissance, suitable control points are selected and their approximate positions are determined. These points provide the reference framework needed for the TLS survey and subsequent comparison of measurements.

One of the important advantages of laser scanning is the possibility of obtaining a point cloud containing a very large number of spatial measurements. Such data can reveal relatively small changes in the shape or position of an object, including local deformations, crack opening, changes in road pavement, displacement of nearby structures and deviations from the expected position of engineering elements. However, raw measurements inevitably contain random noise caused by surface roughness, vegetation, obstacles and other environmental conditions. Data processing therefore requires filtering and calibration. Averaging can reduce random errors, while the use of spatial and temporal information can make the detection of small changes more precise.

The technique was applied to a road as an example of a linear engineering structure. The scanning procedure involves several stages: reconnaissance, planning, selection of controlled objects, development of a scanning scheme, determination of expected measurement accuracy, field preparation, scanning, processing and analysis. If the entire object can be observed from one station, a single scan may be sufficient. Characteristic elements with a regular geometric shape can then be used as virtual control points. Areas with a complex shape or distant sections should receive more detailed scanning.

The position of the scanner has a strong influence on measurement quality. The scanner should be warmed up for approximately fifteen minutes when the air temperature is below 25°C. In normal conditions, controlled areas should generally be within a distance of about 8–60 metres, although favourable conditions may allow a range of approximately 5–80 metres. Greater distances can increase coordinate deviations, and a particularly noticeable growth in error occurs beyond roughly 50–60 metres. For this reason, choosing the scanning distance carefully is essential for reliable results.

Control benchmarks also need to be clearly visible and geometrically well defined. Rigidly fixed objects with a visible scanned area of more than 6 cm² are suitable, while benchmark surfaces larger than about 9 cm² can provide more accurate results. Regular geometric shapes make the centre of a benchmark easier to determine during processing. Additional holes or marks can also help identify the centre from different scanning directions and improve the analysis of deformation.

The angle of incidence is another important factor. It should not exceed approximately 50°, because a larger angle weakens the returning laser signal and may produce uncontrolled errors. Tripod vibration should be minimised, particularly during road surveys, and rubber pads can help stabilise the equipment. Measurements of monitored objects should be repeated at least three times, with a scanning resolution of no less than 10 points per square centimetre. Brightly coloured benchmarks or control areas are preferable because they reduce excessive absorption of the laser rays.

Immediately before scanning, a horizontal and vertical survey network is established at the object. The TLS is then positioned at fixed and coordinated stations, allowing the measurements to be connected to the established geodetic framework. Once the point cloud has been processed, the geometric characteristics and overall shape of the structure can be determined. Repeated surveys can subsequently reveal displacement and deformation over time.

Overall, terrestrial laser scanning provides a detailed spatial description of linear engineering structures and can reduce the amount of conventional geodetic equipment required in the field. Its effectiveness, however, depends on careful planning, suitable control benchmarks, stable scanner positioning, appropriate scanning distances, correct incidence angles and reliable processing of point-cloud data. When these conditions are controlled, TLS can provide a practical basis for monitoring the geometry, deformation and spatial stability of roads and other linear engineering structures.
