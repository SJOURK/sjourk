GPS  

The rapid development of the Global Navigation Satellite System (GNSS) has transformed the positioning landscape. Instead of relying on a single constellation, modern receivers can simultaneously observe GPS, Galileo, GLONASS, and the BeiDou Navigation Satellite System (BDS). This growing constellation of satellites brings clear advantages: better observation geometry, greater signal availability, and improved continuity and reliability of navigation services. Naturally, these benefits have made Precise Point Positioning (PPP) one of the most important techniques for achieving high-precision positioning. However, combining several GNSS constellations is not as straightforward as simply adding more satellites. Each system operates within its own coordinate reference frame, follows a different time reference, and transmits signals with its own technical characteristics. These differences give rise to the Inter-System Bias (ISB)—a seemingly small parameter that can have a significant impact on high-precision positioning and therefore needs to be carefully estimated. 

At its core, PPP determines receiver coordinates directly from raw satellite observations. The mathematical model relies on two fundamental types of measurements: pseudorange and carrier phase. Their accuracy, however, depends heavily on how precisely the geometric distance between the satellite and receiver is represented. The observations are affected by both receiver clock offsets and satellite clock offsets, while the signal itself must travel through the troposphere and ionosphere, each introducing its own delay. The receiver and satellite hardware add another layer of systematic errors, and the carrier phase brings an additional challenge in the form of ambiguity parameters that must be estimated during processing. To keep the remaining errors under control, the PPP model also accounts for antenna characteristics, relativistic effects, Earth rotation, tidal deformation, and phase wind-up. The use of precise satellite orbit and clock products strengthens the model even further by reducing satellite-related uncertainties before the estimation of the remaining parameters begins. 

The positioning strategy considered here is the undifferenced and uncombined (UDUC) PPP model. Rather than forming combinations between frequencies or satellites, UDUC PPP works directly with the original observations. This preserves the full information contained in the measurements while allowing the model to estimate several parameters simultaneously. These include receiver coordinates, atmospheric delays, ambiguity parameters, receiver clock corrections, and ISB values. The treatment of hardware delays is particularly important. Because pseudorange hardware delays are generally stable over time, they can be absorbed into the receiver clock parameter. Similarly, the stable component of the carrier phase hardware delay is incorporated into the ambiguity parameter, while only its time-dependent variation contributes to the receiver clock correction. In this way, the model avoids unnecessarily estimating additional parameters while retaining the precision required for high-accuracy positioning. 

Once several constellations enter the picture, a reference system must be chosen. GPS typically takes this role, providing the reference against which the other systems are compared. Separate ISB parameters are then estimated for Galileo, BDS-2, and BDS-3. Rather than introducing a completely independent receiver clock for every constellation, the model captures the inter-system differences through these additional bias parameters. The BeiDou system deserves particular attention because BDS-2 and BDS-3, despite belonging to the same navigation system, are not fully compatible from an observation-modelling perspective. The modernised BDS-3 constellation introduced changes in signal modulation and receiver processing. Consequently, an additional bias must be estimated between the two generations. In other words, sharing the same system name does not necessarily mean that the receiver sees both generations in exactly the same way. 

The physical origin of ISB is equally interesting. It is not produced by one isolated effect but rather by several physical and technical contributions acting together. One component is associated with differences between system time references, while another originates from receiver hardware delays introduced during signal reception. Signals from different constellations are transmitted at different carrier frequencies and occupy different bandwidths. Inside the receiver, they therefore pass through different electronic channels, filters, and signal-processing paths. These hardware-dependent paths can introduce system-specific delays that remain even when a common GPS time reference is used for precise orbit and clock products. ISB can consequently be viewed as the remaining difference between receiver clock estimates after the principal hardware effects have already been incorporated into the observation model. 

Estimating ISB also requires a decision about how its temporal behaviour should be represented. Several stochastic models are available, each telling a slightly different story about how the parameter evolves. A white noise model assumes that consecutive estimates are statistically independent and fluctuate randomly around a constant mean value. A random walk model, on the other hand, allows the parameter to drift gradually with time, making it suitable when slow changes in the receiver or observation environment are expected. At the opposite extreme, a random constant model assumes that no temporal variation occurs at all. Among these options, the white noise model provides a practical compromise. It offers enough flexibility to accommodate small changes in ISB while avoiding excessive freedom that could compromise numerical stability during continuous processing. 

Long-term PPP introduces another challenge: day-boundary discontinuities (DBD). Precise orbit and clock products are generated separately for individual days, meaning that moving from one day to the next can potentially introduce artificial jumps into the estimated parameters. Maintaining a continuous solution therefore requires careful parameter transfer. Receiver clock estimates, atmospheric parameters, ambiguity values, and ISB estimates are carried forward from one day to the next. Additional constraints are applied to reduce the impact of satellite clock discontinuities, while observations from low-elevation satellites are assigned lower weights because they are generally more vulnerable to atmospheric and multipath effects. Robust estimation methods provide another layer of protection by suppressing observations associated with unusually large residuals. With these measures in place, the remaining influence of DBD on both positioning accuracy and ISB becomes negligible. 

The behaviour of ISB becomes particularly revealing when different observation periods are examined. Over a single day, the parameter is remarkably stable, showing only a small standard deviation (STD) and limited fluctuation. Galileo generally exhibits the most stable behaviour, reflecting its high-quality signals, while BDS-3 shows slightly greater variability. BDS-2 displays the largest variability among the analysed constellations. Interestingly, the choice of precise orbit and clock products can change the absolute ISB values because different products are generated using different processing strategies. However, despite these differences in absolute magnitude, the short-term temporal pattern remains broadly similar. Extend the observation period from one day to several weeks, however, and the picture changes: ISB begins to show more noticeable variations, demonstrating that long-term temporal behaviour cannot simply be ignored in continuous PPP applications. 

Receiver characteristics add another piece to the puzzle. Different manufacturers use different hardware architectures and signal-processing strategies, and these differences inevitably influence the absolute value of the estimated ISB. The receiver antenna also contributes additional hardware delays, introducing further variations between configurations. Yet the story becomes surprisingly consistent when continuous multi-day processing is considered. Stations equipped with different receivers and antennas can exhibit very similar temporal behaviour. Although the absolute magnitude of ISB may change from one receiver configuration to another, its overall pattern of short-term and long-term fluctuations remains remarkably consistent. The receiver therefore influences how large the bias appears, but not necessarily how it behaves over time. 

Ultimately, accurate modelling of ISB is essential for reliable multi-GNSS PPP. What initially appears to be a small correction parameter is actually the product of a complex interaction between receiver hardware, system time references, signal characteristics, observation modelling, atmospheric corrections, ambiguity estimation, and stochastic parameterisation. Understanding these interactions allows the advantages of multiple GNSS constellations to be exploited without allowing their differences to undermine positioning performance. Careful treatment of ISB therefore contributes not only to improved positioning accuracy, but also to more robust Positioning, Navigation, and Timing (PNT) services. As modern GNSS continues to evolve, the ability to combine increasingly diverse satellite signals into a single precise and reliable solution will remain at the heart of high-precision navigation.
