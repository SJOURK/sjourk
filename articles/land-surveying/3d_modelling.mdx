<DropZone answer="LiDAR"/> A remote-sensing method that fires rapid laser pulses at a surface to measure distance and produce highly accurate elevation data.

<DropZone answer="georeferencing"/> The process of assigning real-world geographic coordinates to spatial data so that it aligns correctly with other datasets.

<DropZone answer="point cloud"/> A large set of data points in three-dimensional space, typically produced by laser scanning, that represents the external surface of an object or a piece of terrain.

<DropZone answer="total station"/> A surveying instrument that combines an electronic theodolite with a distance-measuring device to record angles and distances simultaneously.

<DropZone answer="orthophoto"/>  An aerial photograph that has been geometrically corrected so that its scale is uniform, allowing accurate measurement of real-world distances directly from the image.

<DropZone answer="triangulated irregular network"/> A surface made from interconnected triangles whose vertices represent measured points.

<DropZone answer="triangulation"/>  A technique for fixing the position of a point by forming a network of triangles from points whose locations are already known, based on measured angles.

<DropZone answer="deviation analysis"/> AComparing a model with measured data to identify differences.

<DropZone answer="contour line"/> A line on a map or plan connecting points of equal elevation, used to represent the shape and steepness of the land.

<DropZone answer="digital terrain model"/> A digital representation of the bare ground surface, excluding vegetation, buildings and other man-made or natural features.

<DropZone answer="breakline"/> A line representing a significant change in terrain slope or elevation.

<DropZone answer="digital surface model"/> A model representing the terrain together with surface features such as buildings, trees, and other structures.

<DropZone answer="clash detection"/> Identifying geometric conflicts between different elements or models.

<DropZone answer="modelling tolerance"/> The permitted difference between the model and the measured data.

<DropZone answer="level of detail"/> The degree of geometric detail included in a model.

<DropZone answer="cut and fill"/> The removal and addition of material required to achieve a proposed surface.

Optimized 3D Cultural Heritage Models for Web, AR, MR, and VR Applications
Modern smart cultural heritage services use digital technologies to make historical buildings more accessible, interactive, and engaging. Information and communication technology, mobile applications, and advanced visualization methods such as augmented reality (AR), mixed reality (MR), and virtual reality (VR) make it possible to present cultural heritage beyond traditional museums or physical visits. Location-based services (LBS) can connect digital information with a particular place, while 3D models allow users to explore historical objects from different distances and perspectives. This is especially useful for tourists and local communities, as digital content can be accessed when and where it is needed. However, creating reliable and up-to-date digital content for cultural heritage remains technically demanding because advanced 3D modelling is often expensive, time-consuming, and dependent on specialist knowledge. 

Historic Building Information Modelling (HBIM) provides a framework for managing information about existing and historical buildings. It connects specialists from different fields and supports the integration of geometric and non-geometric information throughout the building life cycle. A typical digital documentation workflow consists of three main stages: acquiring raw data, processing the data, and generating the model. Data can come from existing drawings and photographs, traditional surveying, photogrammetric imagery, aerial laser scanning, UAV/UAS platforms, terrestrial laser scanning (TLS), and non-destructive testing. Before modelling, the collected information normally requires registration, segmentation or filtering, triangulation, texturing, and orthophoto generation. The resulting point cloud can then be used for direct, parametric, or hybrid modelling. 

UAV-based photogrammetry offers a relatively inexpensive way to acquire detailed spatial information about historical buildings, particularly when some parts of a structure are difficult or dangerous to reach. UAVs equipped with RGB cameras can produce dense point clouds, digital surface models, orthophotos, and textured 3D models. Ground-based photographs can complement aerial imagery and improve the reconstruction of façades and other areas hidden from the UAV. Terrestrial laser scanning provides another highly accurate source of geometric information. Combining UAV and TLS data can therefore improve coverage, although TLS may have difficulties capturing roofs or other elevated surfaces from ground level. 

The quality of the final model depends strongly on the planning of data acquisition. UAV flights should avoid strong wind and rain, while cloudy conditions around midday are useful because they reduce strong and extended shadows. Several orbital flights at different heights and camera angles can improve the completeness of the reconstruction. After image acquisition, photogrammetric processing involves image orientation, self-calibrating bundle adjustment, feature-based matching, and the generation of an ultra-dense point cloud. In a comparable TLS workflow, scans from different stations are registered into a common coordinate system using control targets and alignment procedures. Point-cloud comparison can then be performed with cloud-to-cloud methods. Fine registration may use the Iterative Closest Point (ICP) algorithm, which minimizes distances between corresponding points. 

A major difficulty appears when a highly detailed textured mesh is prepared for real-time visualization. Raw meshes generated from UAV data may contain millions of polygons and can therefore be too demanding for smartphones, tablets, AR glasses, MR headsets, or other devices with limited computing resources. The solution is mesh optimization, in which the original high-poly model is simplified while its visual quality is preserved as much as possible. The appropriate polygon count depends on the target platform, software architecture, and whether the model is stored locally or delivered through a network. 

A useful strategy is to create several Levels of Detail (LOD) from one high-quality reference model. In this approach, a reference mesh containing around 10 million triangles can be progressively reduced to approximately 3.5 million, 1 million, 250,000, 100,000, and 25,000 triangles. The high-poly model provides detailed geometric information, while lower-poly versions are designed for applications with more limited processing capacity. Instead of keeping all geometric detail in the simplified mesh, a normal map can transfer surface information from the high-poly model. Texture information can also be transferred to the low-poly model, and a diffuse map can then provide its visible surface appearance. UV mapping is used to organise the texture coordinates so that the texture fits correctly onto the simplified geometry. The resulting mesh can be adjusted in Blender before being imported into the Unity game engine. 

Optimization is not simply a matter of reducing the number of triangles. The number of separate objects and draw calls can also have a major influence on performance. In many situations, one complex mesh is more efficient than numerous small meshes. For mobile devices, approximately 25,000–50,000 polygons can be an appropriate range, while Web applications may require fewer than about 250,000 polygons. Desktop applications can support considerably more geometry, depending on the complete scene and hardware. These values should be treated as practical guidelines rather than universal limits. 

The optimized models can support several forms of interaction. In a WebGIS environment, a building can be displayed as a 3D object connected to geographic and historical information. In mobile AR, markerless or superimposition-based techniques can place a life-size model into the physical environment. ARCore can track the device and help it understand surrounding surfaces, allowing users to walk around a virtual building or even view its interior. Several models can also be displayed simultaneously to reveal changes in a building over different periods. 

MR provides another level of interaction by placing a holographic model within the user's environment. Devices such as HoloLens allow users to rotate, move, and scale virtual buildings through gestures or external controllers. VR, in contrast, can provide a more immersive experience by placing the user inside a completely digital environment. 

The use of multiple LOD models is particularly important because the appropriate level of detail can change as the user moves closer to or farther from an object. Switching automatically between LODs reduces unnecessary processing and helps maintain a stable frame rate. This makes detailed cultural heritage models practical across different platforms, from desktop computers to mobile AR applications and MR headsets. 

Digital reconstruction also makes it possible to represent the changing appearance of historical buildings. Instead of keeping only an "As-is” model, a digital database can contain different historical states, including the "As-build” condition and later "As-was-in-year” stages. Users can compare these versions and observe additions, reconstructions, or changes in architectural form. In this way, 3D visualization becomes more than a realistic representation: it becomes a tool for communicating architectural history. 

The overall workflow connects low-cost UAV data acquisition with point-cloud processing, 3D reconstruction, mesh optimization, texturing, and real-time visualization. The resulting digital assets can support cultural tourism, heritage documentation, conservation, condition assessment, restoration planning, and interactive storytelling. By combining accurate geometric data with optimized textures and LOD models, complex historical buildings can be presented effectively on Web3D, AR, MR, and VR platforms without requiring every device to process the original high-density model.
