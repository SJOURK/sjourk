The Cadastre and the Register: Two Names, One Unfinished Story

In Poland, few administrative words cause as much quiet confusion as "cadastre." Surveyors debate it in professional circles, and whenever it enters the public space, people nervously wonder whether a cadastral tax is lurking around the corner. Across the European Union, the real estate cadastre is treated as the foundation of spatial information and a trusted means of verifying ownership. In Poland, however, it is commonly (and often incorrectly) identified with the land and buildings register. The legislator has even declared that, until the register is transformed into a proper cadastre, the two terms should be understood as synonymous. Nobody has said when that transformation will actually happen.

The story begins long before modern bureaucracy. The oldest civilisations already described land in order to protect property rights, and these descriptions gradually evolved into registers of land and its owners, which served as the basis for calculating land tax. The word itself comes from Latin. Emperor Augustus introduced caput iugum, a unit of taxation, alongside capitum registrum, a tax inventory. Over time the two merged into capitastrum and later catastrum, a form close to today's "cadastre." In 1853, the International Statistical Congress in Brussels brought order to the field by setting out principles for measurement, map scales, the drafting of borders and the recording of land use.

Today the International Federation of Surveyors describes the cadastre as an up-to-date, parcel-based land information system. It holds a register of rights to plots of land, usually supported by a geometric description of each plot and often enriched with details about land value. Such a system may serve fiscal purposes like objective taxation, legal purposes such as the transfer of ownership, or broader goals like spatial management and environmental protection. A modern European cadastre combines a graphic part, which shows the geometry of every plot on analogue or digital maps, with a descriptive part that stores the attributes of those geometric objects.

Poland's cadastral history was as turbulent as the country itself. During the partitions, Austrian, Prussian and Russian land tax cadastres operated side by side, and their source materials are still consulted today. The last normative definition came with the 1947 Decree, which described the cadastre as a map-based list and description of land and buildings that could be separate objects of ownership. For each rural district or municipality, officials compiled a cadastral operate from maps, registers and files, recording the class and usage of land, the materials of buildings and even the names of the owner's parents. These operates counted as public documents, so anyone willing to pay could obtain an extract. Courts and surveying authorities had to inform one another ex officio of any changes within thirty days, and owners who failed to report alterations within six weeks risked a fine. Crucially, boundaries were measured by independent, state-certified "sworn" surveyors.

Then ideology intervened. As socialist thinking spread, private property gave way to social property, especially regarding the means of production, while personal property survived only in a limited form. An institution designed to secure property rights had no place in this reality, so in 1955 the cadastre was replaced by the land and buildings register. State-employed surveyors now drew boundaries according to current use, as indicated by interested parties, without requiring any documents, and some limits were even traced from photomaps of doubtful accuracy. The vague notion of "administering" land opened the door to infringements and, at times, outright appropriation of property. Unsurprisingly, the register became an unreliable source of information, and in many respects it still is.

The 1989 Geodesy and Cartography Law abolished the old decree and redefined the register as a uniform, countrywide and regularly updated collection of information on land, buildings, premises and their owners. Record keeping passed to district governors, and the register was digitised and run through ICT systems that shared a common data model. Successive executive regulations reshaped almost every element, from the registry unit to the attributes of plots and buildings, and information on property value was eventually added.

Yet the terminology remains tangled. The Law on Real Estate Management speaks only of the cadastre, which it says establishes the cadastral value of a property. The Law on Land and Mortgage Register relies on cadastral data when entering real estate into land registers, which carry the guarantee of public faith. It also allows a district court to rectify a property's designation at the request of its owner or perpetual user.

So does Poland actually have a real estate cadastre? Technically, its register is not radically different from those elsewhere in the EU. Renaming it would bring welcome consistency, but only if paired with a fundamental change in the data model. Under that change, the property itself would become the basic unit, rather than plots, buildings and premises grouped into record units. For now, neither a scope nor a timeline for this transformation exists.
