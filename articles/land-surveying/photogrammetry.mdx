Land Surveying with UAV Photogrammetry and LiDAR 

The construction industry represents over 9% of most countries’ GDP but continues to face low productivity compared to other sectors. New technologies are helping address this issue, with unmanned aerial vehicles (UAVs) emerging as powerful tools in land surveying and civil engineering. Drones are cost-effective, flexible, and capable of capturing high-resolution aerial images for three-dimensional (3D) models and topographic maps. 

 

Traditional ground surveys using total stations (TS), GNSS, or terrestrial laser scanners remain precise but are time-consuming and labour-intensive. UAV-based methods, particularly photogrammetry and LiDAR, now offer faster, more detailed, and broader spatial coverage. These technologies are increasingly combined to produce accurate digital models for construction planning and environmental monitoring. 

 

Definition of LiDAR 

Light Detection and Ranging (LiDAR) is a remote sensing technology that measures distances by sending out laser pulses and calculating the time it takes for them to return. This produces highly accurate point clouds that can be used to create Digital Terrain Models (DTMs) and Digital Surface Models (DSMs). A key advantage of LiDAR is its ability to penetrate vegetation and capture ground elevation, even in complex environments. It works day or night and provides high precision, though the technology is often expensive and requires advanced data processing skills. 

 

Photogrammetry vs. LiDAR 

Photogrammetry reconstructs 3D models from overlapping images. When performed from UAVs (aerial photogrammetry), it delivers high-resolution surface details, especially for buildings and other visible structures. Its accuracy depends on lighting, texture, and ground control points (GCPs). However, it cannot see through vegetation, meaning that only DSMs, not true ground models, are produced. 

LiDAR, by contrast, directly measures distances with laser pulses. It can penetrate vegetation, making it ideal for generating DTMs in forested or overgrown areas. While LiDAR sensors are more costly, their data provides higher vertical accuracy in natural terrains. 

 

Aerial vs. Terrestrial Photogrammetry 

Photogrammetry can be conducted from the air or from the ground: 

Aerial photogrammetry uses drones or aircraft to capture wide areas from above. It is excellent for large-scale mapping, urban planning, and monitoring construction progress. Its main advantage is speed and coverage, but it can struggle with vertical accuracy if GCPs are missing or lighting conditions are poor. 

Terrestrial photogrammetry involves taking photos from ground level using cameras on tripods or handheld devices. It is more precise for small objects or detailed architectural surveys, as it captures close-range textures and fine details. However, it covers smaller areas and is slower than aerial surveys. 

Together, these methods complement each other: aerial photogrammetry provides broader spatial context, while terrestrial photogrammetry delivers finer detail for specific structures. 

 

Data Integration: Photogrammetry and LiDAR 

Both photogrammetry and LiDAR offer unique strengths. Photogrammetry excels in capturing human-made structures with texture and colour, while LiDAR is better at mapping natural terrains hidden by vegetation. Recent research suggests that combining both technologies produces the most accurate results. 

One promising approach is the Digital Feature Model (DFM), which merges UAV photogrammetry and LiDAR data. In this model, LiDAR provides accurate DTMs in vegetated areas, while photogrammetry supplies DSMs of buildings and infrastructures. The result is a comprehensive elevation model that benefits construction planning, GIS analysis, and environmental monitoring. 

 

Conclusion 

Modern construction and land surveying increasingly rely on UAVs equipped with photogrammetry and LiDAR sensors. Each method has advantages and limitations, but when integrated, they provide accurate, cost-effective, and comprehensive mapping solutions. Aerial photogrammetry captures large areas efficiently, terrestrial photogrammetry adds detailed local precision, and LiDAR ensures reliable ground measurements under vegetation. 

The integration of these technologies supports the creation of Digital Feature Models, enabling precise topographic mapping, improved GIS analysis, and better decision-making for construction and urban planning.
