Match the types of maps to the descriptions.
<DragAndDrop words="physical;political;road;thematic;topographic"> 

<DropZone answer="political"/> Sometimes called a reference map, this type highlights borders between countries, states or cities, along with capitals and major bodies of water. Because its main purpose is to show boundaries rather than landscape, it is usually the first kind of map that students encounter at school.

<DropZone answer="thematic"/> Rather than giving a general overview, it is built around a single subject, such as population density, climate or income. It often uses colour shading (a technique known as a choropleth) to show how the chosen variable changes from one area to another, making spatial patterns easy to compare at a glance.

<DropZone answer="physical"/> This type focuses on the Earth's natural landscape — mountains, plains, rivers and oceans — usually shown through a colour scheme running from green at low elevations, through brown, to grey or white at the highest peaks. Cultural or political information is only included for reference, not as the main focus.

<DropZone answer="topographic"/> A large-scale, highly detailed map that shows relief by means of contour lines — lines joining points of equal elevation. It combines natural features, such as rivers and forests, with man-made ones, such as buildings and roads, which makes them ideal for hiking, civil engineering and geographical planning.

<DropZone answer="road"/> Also called a route map, it shows transport links, together with political boundaries and points of interest such as stations, parks and airports. Minor routes are usually drawn with thin lines, while major highways stand out in a bold colour. It also appears as a bound collection or an atlas.
