Types of Maps

Maps can be classified in several ways, beginning with the medium through which they are presented. A tangible map is a physical map that can be held, stored, and transported, such as a paper map. One of its main advantages is that it does not require specialised hardware, software, or internet access. However, a tangible map represents information from a particular point in time, so its currentness may become uncertain as places and conditions change.

A virtual map, in contrast, is displayed on a computing device such as a desktop computer, tablet, laptop, smartphone, GPS receiver, or another digital device. Virtual maps can be updated easily and may be dynamic, meaning that their content can change or develop while the user is viewing it. They can also include animation and connect the user to additional information, including documents, photographs, videos, and sounds. Digital maps are therefore easy to share and can contain much more information than a traditional paper map. Their disadvantages include the need for appropriate hardware and software, regular maintenance, and sometimes greater technical expertise. They may also be less intuitive for users who are unfamiliar with digital mapping systems.

A mental map is different because it exists in a person's mind as a conceptualization of space. People use mental maps when travelling, planning everyday activities, or trying to understand where an event has happened. These internal representations also reflect a person's geographic knowledge and spatial awareness. Since mental maps are personal, two people rarely imagine the same place in exactly the same way. A mental map can only be communicated to another person when it is transformed into another form, such as a drawing, a digital map, speech, or writing.

Maps can also be divided according to their purpose and the type of information they emphasise. A reference map, sometimes called a general reference map, provides a broad representation of geographic reality. It concentrates on the location of different features and usually identifies them with labels. Roads, rivers, settlements, boundaries, and other geographic features are generally represented with relatively equal importance. Reference maps are not designed for one specialised purpose, so they can support a wide range of activities. Topographic maps and image maps created from satellite or aircraft data are common examples.

A thematic map has a more specific purpose because it focuses on a particular subject or theme. Instead of presenting all geographic features equally, it highlights an attribute and shows how that attribute varies across space. A thematic map may focus on one theme, although a multivariate thematic map can represent several related attributes at the same time. Features that are not relevant to the theme are often left out. For instance, a map showing election results may display administrative boundaries while excluding highways and rivers because they do not contribute directly to the subject being represented.

One special type of thematic or purpose-oriented map is a pragmatic map, which is designed to guide spatial behaviour. Its main function is to help people decide how to move from one location to another. Such a map deliberately removes unnecessary information and may simplify or generalize geographic features. A transport network map is a typical example, while aeronautical charts and nautical charts help people navigate through the air or across water. Another specialised type is a persuasive map, also known as a propaganda map. Rather than simply presenting geographic information, it is designed to influence the reader's beliefs or opinions about a subject or event. It may use selective information, dramatic symbols, or exaggerated representations to create a particular impression.

Thematic maps can be divided into several important categories. A choropleth map assigns a colour to each enumeration unit, such as a county, according to a particular value or range of values. Data are usually divided into classes, allowing differences between areas to be seen quickly. A dot density map represents quantitative information with dots placed within enumeration units. Each dot corresponds to a particular quantity, so a larger number of dots indicates a higher value.

A proportional symbol map uses symbols whose size is directly related to the value of an attribute. A larger symbol therefore represents a larger quantity, making it possible to compare magnitudes between different locations. A graduated symbol map also uses symbol size, but the symbols represent ranges or classes of values rather than being directly proportional to the data. The symbols therefore show the order or category of values rather than their exact proportional magnitude.

A flow map focuses on movement between locations. Linear symbols can represent the movement of goods, money, technology, people, or other phenomena. Differences in the thickness, size, or classification of symbols can communicate the magnitude or order of the flows. Colour may also be used to distinguish different routes, time periods, or categories of movement.

Finally, a cartogram changes the size of geographic areas according to the value of a particular attribute rather than preserving their actual geographical area. An enumeration unit with a relatively high value becomes enlarged, while one with a lower value becomes smaller. This process can significantly distort the original shapes of countries, regions, or other areas. Contiguous cartograms keep the areas connected, whereas non-contiguous cartograms separate them while generally preserving their original shapes. Although this distortion can make geographical forms less recognisable, it allows the reader to compare the relative importance of areas according to the chosen data.

<DragAndDrop words="cadastral;choropleth;geological;physical;political;road;thematic;topographic"> 

<DropZone answer="political"/> Sometimes called a reference map, this type highlights borders between countries, states or cities, along with capitals and major bodies of water. Because its main purpose is to show boundaries rather than landscape, it is usually the first kind of map that students encounter at school.

<DropZone answer="thematic"/> Rather than giving a general overview, it is built around a single subject, such as population density, climate or income. It often uses colour shading (a technique known as a choropleth) to show how the chosen variable changes from one area to another, making spatial patterns easy to compare at a glance.

<DropZone answer="physical"/> This type focuses on the Earth's natural landscape — mountains, plains, rivers and oceans — usually shown through a colour scheme running from green at low elevations, through brown, to grey or white at the highest peaks. Cultural or political information is only included for reference, not as the main focus.

<DropZone answer="cadastral"/> A map that shows the boundaries and ownership of land parcels, typically used for legal, administrative, and taxation purposes. It records property lines, parcel numbers, land area, and sometimes ownership details. Such maps are maintained by government land registries and are essential for property surveys, land transactions, and urban planning.

<DropZone answer="topographic"/> A large-scale, highly detailed map that shows relief by means of contour lines — lines joining points of equal elevation. It combines natural features, such as rivers and forests, with man-made ones, such as buildings and roads, which makes them ideal for hiking, civil engineering and geographical planning.

<DropZone answer="road"/> Also called a route map, it shows transport links, together with political boundaries and points of interest such as stations, parks and airports. Minor routes are usually drawn with thin lines, while major highways stand out in a bold colour. It also appears as a bound collection or an atlas.

<DropZone answer="choropleth"/> A type of a thematic map that uses shading, coloring, or patterns applied to predefined areas (such as countries, states, counties, or census tracts) to represent the value of a statistical variable across those areas. Darker or more intense colors typically indicate higher values. Common uses include showing population density, election results, income levels, or disease rates by region.

 <DropZone answer="geological"/> A map that shows the distribution, types, and structures of rocks and geological features at or near the Earth's surface. It typically depicts rock formations by age and composition, fault lines, folds, mineral deposits, and other subsurface structures. Such maps are used in mining, construction, resource exploration, and hazard assessment (e.g., identifying earthquake fault zones).
